A decision-maker has three to four trade show days a year, rarely more. The real question is not which shows exist in your sector, but which ones deserve two days out of your office. This guide gives you a method for deciding, not another list. Here are the six criteria we apply at Agoris to evaluate a show from the visitor's point of view.

## 1. Does the Show Really Speak to Your Role?

The same sector can host several shows that do not target the same people. Some address procurement, others technical managers, others still senior executives. Before booking, check the announced profile of visitors and exhibitors: a show where you will meet neither your peers nor your suppliers holds no interest, however prestigious it is. A fully professional, well-targeted audience is worth more than a large generalist crowd.

## 2. The Density of Relevant Exhibitors, Not the Total Number

A show with 1,000 exhibitors is not necessarily more useful than one with 200 if only a handful of stands match your need. Browse the exhibitor list from the previous edition, almost always available online, and count those you would genuinely go and see. If you find fewer than ten, the trip is worth questioning. This is often where specialised shows beat large generalist ones: fewer aisles to walk, but a much higher concentration of relevant contacts.

## 3. Is the Conference Programme Worth the Trip on Its Own?

For many visitors, conferences and workshops justify the visit by themselves: regulatory updates, case studies, sector trends. Look at the previous edition's programme to judge the real calibre of speakers. A line-up of recognised executives and experts is a sign of quality; a string of sales pitches dressed up as conferences is a much weaker one.

## 4. Frequency and Trajectory: Is the Show in Good Shape?

A well-established annual show whose attendance grows edition after edition is a safer bet than a recent or declining event. Look for the results of recent editions: a show that proudly publishes its figures and sees them rise is generally one that attracts the right exhibitors. Conversely, an organiser who publishes no results at all invites caution. French trade show attendance has broadly picked up in recent years: a show that is not riding this general momentum deserves a closer look at its own health.

## 5. The Real Cost of the Trip, Time Included

Visiting a show is free or nearly so, but the trip is not: transport, a hotel night where needed, and above all two days out of the office for you and your team. This opportunity cost is the real price of the visit. A nearby show, easily reached by public transport, where you wrap up your meetings in a single day, can be more profitable than a distant flagship event that ties up an entire team for three days. Factor in the venue too: a well-connected, well-organised exhibition centre saves precious time compared with a poorly linked or maze-like site.

## 6. A Clear Objective, or Do Not Come

The last criterion is not about the show but about you. Before booking, spell out what you are coming to achieve: source three specific suppliers, attend two conferences, meet an identified partner. A visitor with no objective leaves a show with a bag of freebies and nothing else. A visitor who knows why they are there makes the day pay off, even at a small show.

## In Summary

Choosing a show to visit comes down to weighing six factors: fit with your role, density of relevant exhibitors, quality of the programme, the show's trajectory, the real cost of the trip, and the clarity of your objective. No show ticks all six boxes perfectly, but a show that fails three or four of them probably is not worth your time.
