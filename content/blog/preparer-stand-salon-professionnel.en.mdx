A failed stand is rarely decided during the show itself: it is decided months earlier, sometimes years, in whatever was forgotten or left unplanned. Most available checklists start three months before the show, which is already far too late for the most structuring decisions. Here is a realistic countdown, calibrated on the real timelines of the field.

## At the Previous Edition: Book Your Space

This is the first surprise for a first-time exhibitor: at major trade shows, the best locations are booked during the current edition, for the next one. Organisers offer exhibitors already on site the chance to renew and choose their spot before general public registration opens. In practical terms, depending on the event's frequency, this means committing a year ahead for an annual show, and two to three years ahead for a biennial or triennial one. If you are after a strategic location (a corner, a main aisle, proximity to a busy hub), this is when it is decided, not three months before.

The flip side, for those not yet exhibiting: visit the show beforehand, meet the organiser's sales team on site, and position yourself at this edition for the next one. Waiting for public registration to open means accepting whatever is left.

## About a Year Out: Strategic Planning and Hotels

Two workstreams at this point. First, the planning: set your objectives with all stakeholders, not just marketing. Sales objectives (leads, meetings, targeted revenue), marketing objectives (brand awareness, product launch, content), and, where relevant, the objectives of product, HR or communications teams need to be agreed jointly and in writing. A show prepared by a single department invariably produces conflicting expectations and a disputed post-show review. This planning determines the overall budget, the required floor space and team size.

Second, accommodation. At major international shows, hotels near the exhibition centre get booked up to a year in advance, and rates climb as availability shrinks. Block your rooms as soon as the dates are confirmed, adjusting the number later if needed. This is one of the areas where planning ahead saves the most money, with no effort at all.

## Six to Three Months Out: Choose Your Contractor and Design the Stand

This is the real timeline for a quality stand. Allow six months if you are going bespoke: you need to consult several stand-building contractors, compare proposals, choose one, then leave time for design, back-and-forth on the layout, and manufacturing. Three months is an absolute minimum, and only for a modular or packaged stand. Below that, you take whatever is available, at a premium, from contractors already overloaded by other exhibitors at the same show.

At this stage: brief the contractor, sign off on the design, order the fit-out. Also book technical services with the organiser (electricity and power, internet, furniture, cleaning, carpeting), reading carefully what is or is not included in the regulations, as this varies considerably from one show to another. Plan ahead for transport and assembly of your equipment.

## Three to One Month Out: Team and Materials

Assemble and brief your stand team: who is coming, for what purpose, with what messaging and what individual objectives. Produce your materials: documentation, signage and point-of-sale displays, samples, any gifts, demonstrations to prepare. Check printing and production lead times, which lengthen as major shows approach.

## One Month to One Week Out: Drum Up Business

A stand does not fill itself; it needs commercial groundwork. Communicate about your presence: inform your clients and prospects, invite them, announce it on your social channels and in the show's own communications. Request meetings in advance with the contacts you absolutely want to see: a pre-filled diary beats a stand that just waits. Prepare your lead-capture tool (app, badge scanning, qualification form) so you are not improvising on the day. Finalise the team brief: individual objectives, shift allocation, talking points.

## Show Day and During the Event

Handle badge collection and set-up the day before or early in the morning. On the stand, keep your meetings, qualify and log every useful contact immediately, rather than putting it off until evening when memories blur. Track your indicators day by day (leads collected, meetings held) to adjust if needed between days. And pace yourself: a show is run like a marathon, not a sprint.

## Day+1 to Day+15: The Follow-Up That Makes the Difference

This is the most profitable and most neglected step. Follow up with every useful contact within two weeks, with a personalised message recalling your conversation: send the promised document, propose a time slot, restate their need. A prospect met on a stand should never receive a generic newsletter two weeks later. Beyond that window, the memory effect fades and your leads cool off. Sort your notes, prioritise by level of interest, and feed these contacts into your sales pipeline.

## Day+15 to Day+30: Debrief and Review

Two to four weeks later, bring the team together for a structured debrief while memories are still fresh. Compare results against your objectives, calculate your metrics (cost per lead, meetings secured, early opportunities), identify what worked well and what cost money without paying off. This review feeds two decisions: adjustments for the next show, and above all, deciding, figures in hand, whether it is worth going back next year.

## In Summary

A successful trade show participation runs on a much longer countdown than most people assume: space booked at the previous edition, one to three years ahead depending on frequency; objectives set with every department and hotels booked around a year ahead; contractor chosen and stand designed six to three months ahead; team and materials in the final quarter; commercial groundwork in the last month; then follow-up within two weeks and a debrief within the following month. The most structuring decisions, location and objectives, are made well before most exhibitors even start organising.
